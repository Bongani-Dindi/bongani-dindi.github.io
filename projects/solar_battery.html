01

The project

I am developing a Python workflow that brings Cape Town PV production together with residential electricity-demand profiles. It compares direct solar use, household batteries and shared storage, then summarises the energy flows and economic assumptions behind each scenario.

The work covers data loading, household profiles, storage dispatch, analysis, plotting, optimisation and economics. Recent effort has focused on a foundation that is easy to underestimate: aligning demand and PV on a continuous calendar while retaining the distinction between measured, repeated and estimated data. The documented pipeline is a study tool; forecast validation and scenario interpretation are ongoing.

02

Project goals

  1. Prepare trustworthy time series. Make household demand and PV timestamps compatible, check source coverage and identities, and record any estimated or repeated values.
  2. Compare storage behaviour. Evaluate local and shared-storage scenarios while enforcing battery capacity, charge/discharge power and efficiency constraints.
  3. Explain the energy and economic results. Report grid demand, solar use and storage histories with clear aggregation rules, then connect them to explicit cost and tariff assumptions.
  4. Evaluate forecasting separately. Compare proposed forecasts with observed data and test how forecast errors affect storage decisions before drawing conclusions about predictive control.
03

Technical learning

Calendar alignment and leap-day treatment

Matching the number of rows does not establish that demand and solar describe the same hours. I am learning to inspect calendar months, hour labels and leap years before combining data. The current loader includes a helper that assigns repeated non-leap demand profiles to the solar calendar and estimates each 29 February hour from the same hour on 28 February and 1 March. It marks those rows with a load-imputation flag.

Source identity and missing-data limits

Household column names can remain unchanged while the underlying measured households differ between source years. The loader checks available profile metadata, coverage and household identities. Work on unresolved gaps has also shown why long gaps and absent year boundaries need explicit handling. A repeated source profile is a study assumption that must remain visible in the interpretation.

Power, energy and storage accounting

The pipeline has helped me distinguish a power rating from energy transferred during a timestep, and stored energy from energy delivered after losses. Battery dispatch needs both capacity and rate limits. In the shared system, physical stored energy and household billing credits are separate accounts. Their balances and aggregation rules need to be explained before comparing scenarios.

Look-ahead control, forecasting and economics

The documented precharging controller uses a look-ahead rule to estimate upcoming shortages from the supplied time series. This has prompted a separate question: how would it perform using forecasts available at the decision time? The economics and optimisation work also requires clear assumptions about installed costs, operating costs, tariffs, replacements and the metric being optimised.

04

Soft skills & working habits

  • Debugging through evidence. Inspecting source years, file paths, index lengths and missing timestamps has helped me narrow a large pipeline problem to specific assumptions. I am learning to describe the cause of a failure and the evidence needed to fix it.
  • Keeping modelling decisions traceable. Decisions about repeating a source year, interpolating a gap or estimating leap-day load materially affect the study. Recording those choices alongside the data makes the work easier to review and prevents a convenient implementation from becoming an unstated assumption.
  • Explaining results to a broader audience. Working through the analysis, optimiser and economics has helped me connect a technical change to its effect on storage operation or scenario cost. Clear plots, units and assumptions make the comparison useful to readers who do not work in the code.
05

Detailed updates

A record of documented work and learning through September 2026. Future verification and delivery steps are listed separately.

30 September 2026

An explicit calendar and imputation helper

The supplied data-loading source contains a dedicated helper for keeping a complete target calendar, inserting leap-day demand estimates and flagging them. It checks target ordering, uniqueness, source length and missing neighbour values before returning aligned demand.

Learning: retain measured solar values on their original calendar and identify estimated demand separately. Calendar consistency and data provenance are both needed for a defensible comparison.

September 2026

Source-year and data-quality investigation

Troubleshooting focused on repeated source-year files, household identities and gaps that remained after limited interpolation. The investigation clarified the distinction between extending a study with repeated profiles and claiming a continuous record of the same measured households.

Learning: inspect the source metadata as well as the dataframe shape. A clear error can reveal a study-design issue that a silent fill would hide.

August–September 2026

Understanding dispatch, optimisation and economics

The pipeline and code-reference work traced standalone and shared-storage dispatch, forecast precharging, summary metrics, optimisation and economic calculations. The documentation also covers automated checks for the optimiser and economics modules.

Learning: verify the physical energy boundaries and cost assumptions before interpreting a headline result. Forecast accuracy still needs its own comparison against observed data.
